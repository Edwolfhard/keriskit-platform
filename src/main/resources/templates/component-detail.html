A closer look

Understanding this component

The hulu is the hilt of the keris: the part held in the hand. Its form brings together practical function and artistic expression. Shaped to accommodate a grip, it also offers a surface through which a carver explores proportion, ornament, and the character of the material.

Designed for the Hand

The hulu was shaped to serve as the handle of a weapon. Its design therefore responds to the grasp of the hand, giving its curves and proportions a practical purpose alongside their visual appeal.

When examining a hilt, begin with its overall outline. Follow the transition between the upper section, the grip, and the base. Consider how these parts work together as a form intended to be held.

Form and Cultural Change

Keris hilt design reflects changing cultural and religious influences. Earlier forms described in Malay craft literature include representations associated with Hindu figures and Garuda. The arrival of Islam is described as influencing the adaptation of hilt designs into other forms.

The resulting variety includes designs associated with birds and other familiar shapes. Rather than following a single outline, hilt carving developed through different interpretations of form. These traditions provide context for understanding the hulu without establishing the precise origin or type of the example displayed here.

Materials and the Beauty of Wood

Historical examples of keris hilts were made from a range of materials, including wood, horn, ivory, and metals. Each material offered a different appearance and possibilities for shaping and decoration.

For wooden hilts, the natural grain is an important part of their aesthetic character. Craftspersons selected wood with attention to the beauty and suitability of its grain. Kemuning is among the woods recorded in traditional hilt making, and the preparation of dry wood is also emphasised.

On a finished wooden hilt, smooth areas allow the grain to remain visible, while carved sections introduce additional patterns and texture. The wood species of the displayed example has not been verified.

Ornament and Artistic Expression

Hilt ornament draws on nature and animal forms. Documented decorative approaches include awan larat, raised floral ornament, sculptural work, and turned decoration. These belong to a wider vocabulary of hilt design; every hilt does not carry the same motifs or techniques.

Decoration also interacts with the shape beneath it. A carved band may draw attention around the grip, while a smooth surface makes a change in curvature more visible. Looking at both together helps reveal how the hilt is composed as a complete object.

From Material to Finished Hilt

Carving involves several stages, from shaping the material to defining the ornament and refining its surface. Traditional tools include saws, chisels, and carving knives, each supporting different parts of the process.

Surface finishing receives particular attention. The craft guide records the use of natural smoothing materials, including prepared leaves and stingray skin. These details show that a finished hilt depends on careful preparation and refinement as well as the carving itself.

Details to Observe

Study the silhouette before focusing on the smaller details. Follow the curve from the upper section towards the base, then examine the carved bands and their spacing. Notice where ornament meets a smooth surface and how the wood grain appears beneath the finish.

These observations connect the hilt's practical form with its visual character. Together, its outline, material, and surface treatment offer a closer view of the craft behind the hulu.

The pendokok is the metal collar positioned between the lower end of the hulu and the ganja at the base of the blade. Although small in comparison with the surrounding parts, it contributes a distinctive element to the appearance of the assembled keris.

At the Junction of Hilt and Blade

The pendokok sits around the junction where the blade's tang, known as the puting, enters the hilt. Its position connects it visually with both the hulu above and the blade below.

Related fittings are described using terms such as cincin, kili-kili, and mendak. Terminology varies between traditions, so these names should be understood within the context in which they are used.

Materials and Metalwork

Pendokok are described in a range of metals, including gold, silver, suasa, bronze, and copper. The chosen material, together with its finish and ornament, contributes to the character of the fitting.

Decorative work may involve surface carving, pierced ornament, or casting. These approaches create different relationships between raised details, recessed areas, and open spaces.

The displayed illustration has a silver-coloured surface with dark recesses. Its appearance alone does not establish the metal composition or the technique used to make an equivalent physical object.

Nature in Miniature

The decorative vocabulary associated with pendokok includes ketam guri, lotus flowers, clove flowers, ferns, and curling bean tendrils. Such motifs belong to a wider interest in plant forms within Malay decorative arts.

The supplied account draws comparisons with ornament on wooden architecture, keris hilts and sheaths, batik, and songket. These connections offer a way to consider the pendokok as part of a broader artistic tradition.

In this illustration, observe the floral relief, curling lines, and bead-like borders. The specific traditional names of the illustrated flowers have not been established.

Shape and Regional Traditions

Pendokok occur in different profiles and decorative arrangements. The supplied account names forms including tajung, Bugis Patani, pacat kenyang, teratai, and telur ikan.

It also associates variations with Bugis, Palembang, and Minang traditions, and distinguishes examples connected with Sulawesi, Terengganu, and Kelantan–Pattani. These associations provide context for comparison rather than a definitive identification of the example shown here.

A feature such as an elongated neck may prompt a question about regional style. Establishing origin requires supporting information about the object, rather than relying on one visible feature alone.

Personal Taste and Presentation

When a keris is sheathed, the pendokok can remain visible alongside the hilt and the upper sheath. Its metal surface introduces a different visual quality beside the surrounding wood.

The selection of form, material, and ornament can express the owner's taste and contribute to the presentation of the whole keris. Connections with social standing depend on cultural and historical context; an elaborate fitting alone does not establish an owner's rank.

Follow the arrangement of flowers, curling ornament, and border details. Notice how the bright raised surfaces contrast with darker recesses, giving definition to decoration on a small object.

The sampir is the broad, transverse upper part of the keris sheath. Positioned above the batang serunai, it gives the sheath its distinctive upper silhouette. It is also described as the perahu keris, a name that recalls the outline of a boat.

The Upper Part of the Sheath

The sampir and batang serunai form adjoining parts of the sheath, but they are distinct components. Their colour and grain may create a continuous appearance even when different pieces of wood have been used.

The sampir accommodates the broader area at the base of the blade, including the ganja, while the batang serunai extends below it around the length of the blade. This relationship explains the contrast between the broad upper form and the elongated lower section.

Fit and Protection

The internal shape of the sampir is important to the fit of the sheathed keris. Its cavity must correspond with the blade it receives, helping to protect the broader basal area from unwanted contact.

In the supplied article, Angah Sunan describes this close fit as a form of “lock”. This is best understood here as an account of careful fitting, rather than evidence that every keris contains an identical mechanical locking device. The quality of the fit depends on the individual blade and sheath.

Wood and Craftsmanship

The article describes the selection of harder wood for the sampir and comparatively softer wood for the batang serunai. This is a description of the practice discussed by the author, rather than a rule for every sheath.

Among the woods named for sampir making are kemuning, saga, ciku, and acacia. Selection brings together practical suitability and visual character: the grain, colour, and finish all contribute to the appearance of the completed piece.

The wood species of the illustrated example has not been verified. Its visible grain can be explored as a design feature without assigning it a particular species.

Forms and Regional Traditions

Sampir profiles vary across keris traditions. The article identifies forms including tebeng, sehari bulan or bulan sabit, and tajung Patani. It also discusses Javanese forms such as gayaman and ladrang as points of comparison.

Within the Malay Peninsula, the sampir can be considered alongside the hilt, blade, and other fittings when exploring regional style. A profile can offer clues, but a single component does not establish the origin of an entire keris or the ancestry of its owner.

Inherited objects, gifts, and changes to fittings can bring together elements from different traditions. The illustrated sampir is therefore presented as a component study rather than a confirmed regional type.

The Boat and Maritime Identity

In “Rahsia Sampir Keris: Kod Maritim” (2018), Angah Sunan interprets the boat-like sampir as an expression of connections between Malay culture and the sea. The reading links its outline with voyaging and the maritime setting of the wider archipelago.

This is a symbolic interpretation of the form, rather than a proven account of why every sampir was originally designed. It offers a cultural perspective alongside the component’s practical role as part of the sheath.

The sarung is the sheath that houses the blade of the keris when it is not in use. Its protective purpose is expressed through a carefully shaped exterior, while its wood, proportions, and decoration contribute to the appearance of the complete keris.

A Protective Housing for the Blade

The sarung encloses the blade, helping to protect it from unwanted contact and physical damage. The supplied craft infographic presents this protection as the sheath's principal function when the keris is not being used.

Its external form should therefore be considered together with the blade it contains. The sheath is part of the keris assembly, and its shape must accommodate the form of the blade rather than being chosen for appearance alone.

Understanding the Complete Sheath

This viewer presents the sarung as a complete assembly: the broad sampir at the top, the elongated batang serunai below it, and the terminal piece at the lower end, referred to in this guide as the kasut or buntut.

The sampir forms the wider upper portion, while the batang serunai continues around the length of the blade. The lower terminal completes the outline of the illustrated sheath. Looking at these sections together makes their proportions and relationships easier to understand.

Naming and construction details can vary between traditions. The separate component guides allow each part to be examined more closely, while this page explains their relationship within the whole sarung.

Wood, Grain, and Visual Character

The supplied infographic identifies wood as the material used for the sarung and names kemuning and bacang hutan among the woods associated with its making. These examples describe materials used in the craft, rather than identifying the species shown in this illustration.

Wood grain is an important feature to observe. Variations in colour and pattern can distinguish adjoining sections, even when their finished surfaces form a visually unified sheath. In this illustration, the broad upper part and the long lower section display contrasting grain patterns.

The species and construction of a physical sheath cannot be established from this illustration alone. The viewer is intended to support observation of its visible form.

Designed as Part of the Keris

The infographic explains that sheath design is considered in relation to the blade, hilt, and type of keris. This relationship brings practical fit and visual composition into the same process.

A broad upper section, a long lower body, and a shaped terminal create different visual roles within one object. Their proportions can be studied alongside the hilt and other fittings to understand the appearance of the assembled keris.

Ornament and Regional Variety

Sheath forms and decorative treatments vary across keris traditions. The supplied infographic draws attention to differences in carving and motifs between Javanese and Peninsula examples.

Such comparisons provide a starting point for examining individual objects, rather than a fixed rule for every sheath. A plain surface can reveal the character of the wood, while an ornamented surface places greater emphasis on carved detail. The illustrated example mainly highlights its profile, grain, and polished appearance.

The bilah is the blade of the keris. Its profile, surface, and named features offer a way to understand both the structure of the weapon and the craft involved in its making. The illustrated example presents a straight blade with a broad, asymmetric base tapering towards a pointed tip.

Straight and Wavy Forms

Keris blades occur in straight and wavy forms. Keris Melayu Semenanjung describes changes in blade form over time, alongside the development of different regional traditions. A straight blade is therefore part of the variety of keris forms, rather than an incomplete version of a wavy blade.

Begin by following the outline from the broad base towards the tip. In this illustration, the blade narrows along a largely straight profile. Compare its broad faces with the narrow side views to distinguish width from thickness.

The Base and Its Connection to the Hilt

The puting is the tang that extends from the base of the blade into the hilt. It is visible in this component study because the hilt has been removed from the illustration.

The book identifies the basal element as ganjar, also called aring. Its blade diagrams also name features around the base, including belalai gajah, janggut, and kerawang. These terms describe details found in the examples discussed; they should not all be assigned to the comparatively simple base of the illustrated blade.

Reading the Surface

The anatomical diagrams identify features such as pamor, alur, lurah, and tulang. These names help direct attention to differences in surface pattern and the contours of a blade.

Their presence and visibility vary between examples. Dark colour or a mottled surface alone should not be treated as confirmation of a particular pamor pattern. The photograph and generated views do not establish this blade's metal composition, age, or exact manufacturing process.

Towards the Hujung Tuntung

The book calls the terminal point of the blade the hujung tuntung and explains its importance in the keris's historical role as a weapon. It is a distinct feature to consider when examining the blade's overall proportions.

Observe how the sides converge towards the point and how the profile changes near the end. The illustration supports visual study of this shape; it does not indicate the sharpness or condition of the physical blade.

The Craft Behind the Blade

The book distinguishes several roles in keris production. An empu may undertake work from forging the iron through to completing the hilt and sheath. A pandai besi may specialise in producing and finishing the blade before passing it to makers of the other fittings.

A tukang bekalan is described as preparing and forging the metal into a preliminary blade form for another craftsperson to refine. This division of work shows that the complete keris can bring together several specialised skills.

The kasut, also called the buntut, is the terminal part at the lower end of the keris sheath. It completes the outline of the sarung and forms the finishing point of the batang serunai. Its shape and construction are considered in relation to the rest of the sheath.

Completing the Sheath

Keris Melayu Semenanjung places the kasut at the furthest end of the sarung. It is fitted to the lower end of the batang serunai, completing the appearance of the sheath.

The upper wooden section visible in this illustration shows the adjoining end of the batang serunai. The lower, stepped section allows the transition to the terminal piece to be examined more closely.

Separate and Integral Construction

A kasut may be made as a separate fitting, but this is not the only method described in the book. On keris Tajung examples, the terminal can be formed as part of the batang serunai itself.

For separately fitted examples, the book describes a projecting section or tenon inserted into the batang serunai to make the connection more secure. It also records examples attached at the end using adhesive.

The internal attachment is not visible in this illustration. A line between the sections does not establish which fitting method was used.

A Visual Relationship with the Sampir

The book explains that the kasut is commonly made from the same material as the sampir. This choice helps the upper and lower ends of the sheath appear coordinated.

Colour, grain, and finish can therefore be considered across the whole sarung rather than in isolation. The illustrated example has a polished wooden appearance, but its wood species has not been verified.

Different Profiles and Forms

The examples in the book include thin, rounded, and stepped terminals. It also identifies forms called buntut lipas and buntut belalang, illustrating differences in their outline and proportions.

Bugis examples are described as generally thicker and larger, with some having stepped profiles. The book also records adaptations of Bugis terminals on Peninsula Bugis keris.

These comparisons provide context for the range of forms. The stepped profile in this illustration alone does not confirm a particular regional origin.

Choosing a Form for the Whole

The shape of the kasut is also related to the type of sampir. The book associates some thin terminals with sampir tebeng, while illustrating other forms with sampir sehari bulan or bulan sabit.

It also records variation within these pairings. The relationship is useful for comparison, but it should not be treated as a rigid rule covering every sheath.
